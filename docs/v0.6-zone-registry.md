# v0.6 — the zone registry

The full story of how this idea arrived, what it is, why the obvious version of
it is wrong, and how to build it. Written 2026-07-31 after three false starts,
so the fourth doesn't repeat them.

`docs/BACKLOG.md` points here rather than duplicating it.

---

## 1. How we got here

**The complaint.** Free zones and the home zone are circles you draw by tapping
a map and dragging a radius slider. Wasil's verdict: *"radius very inaccurate"* —
a circle centred on a tap covers the wrong side of the street and half a canal.
He asked whether real parking areas could be tapped instead.

**First wrong turn — polygons.** The instinct was to find polygon boundaries and
run point-in-polygon tests. That is what the app already does for the 29 bundled
Amsterdam tariff areas.

**Second wrong turn — RDW.** A suggestion that the national register (NPR /
SPDP2.0) publishes zone geometry. Checked against the live APIs, twice:

- `PARKEERGEBIED` (`mz4f-59fw`) returns four fields and **no geometry**:
  `areamanagerid`, `areaid`, `usageid`, `uuid`.
- `PARKEERADRES` (`ygq4-hh5q`) holds the **municipality's own postal address** —
  "Postbus 15, Appingedam", a phone number, an email. Where you write to the
  council, not where the parking is.
- Of eight RDW parking datasets, only `GEO Parkeer Garages` and `GEO Carpool`
  carry coordinates, both Point-only and about car parks.
- `npropendata.rdw.nl/parkingdata/v2` indexes parking **facilities** — garages
  and P+R sites — not on-street zones.

**RDW has no coordinates for on-street parking.** Do not propose it again.

**The correction that mattered.** Wasil, after watching two assistants argue
circle-versus-polygon:

> *"Q-Park has a system where it doesn't matter where you are, you can buy that
> place's area code… that is literally what I am trying to recreate."*

He was right and both of us were solving the wrong problem. The pins in a
parking app are not boundaries you are inside or outside. They are **purchasable
reference points**. You never prove you are within zone 12676's true shape — you
find the nearest one and buy it. Containment never enters into it, so the
accuracy of a circle is irrelevant, because there is no circle.

**The find.** Opening the council's own tariff map in a browser and watching its
network traffic showed where its pins come from. Then the official portal turned
out to publish the zones properly.

## 2. The idea

A flat registry of parking zones, each with an identifier and a position.
Given a location — yours or the car's — find the nearest entry. That answer
drives everything: which zone to buy, whether this spot is free, whether it is
home.

```kotlin
fun nearest(point: GeoPoint, zones: List<Zone>): Zone? =
    zones.minByOrNull { haversine(point, it.centre) }
```

No polygon containment. No radius. No geometry question to settle.

---

## 3. The goal, and what it is *not*

**Goal:** one lookup serving three questions that are currently three systems —
"is this home", "is this a free spot", and eventually "which zone do I pay for".

**Not a replacement for today's claim logic.** This distinction has been lost
twice, so state it plainly:

| Question | What answers it |
|---|---|
| Which zone would I buy? | nearest point — this document |
| Does this spot need the permit at all? | paid-versus-free, which the bundled polygons already answer correctly |

The permit is free. There is nothing to buy yet. This registry is the
**foundation of in-app payment** — the public-product direction — not a rewrite
of the permit app.

**Wasil's architecture note**, which shapes the whole thing:

> *"We can make another subsection in the app for later that will handle the
> payments part for people without permits… and then another section for people
> with 1 car / no permit / paying for parking spots."*

Two products over one core. Detect the park → find the nearest zone → act. Only
the final action differs: claim a free permit, or buy a tariff. Build the lookup
once, with both in mind.

## 4. The data

Three sources, and they are not interchangeable.

### 4.1 `parkeerzones` — official, documented, use this for zones

```
https://api.data.amsterdam.nl/v1/parkeerzones/parkeerzones/
```

```json
{
  "id": 1,
  "gebiedscode": "BL04",
  "gebiedsnaam": "West 6",
  "gebiedsomschrijving": "BL04 West 6",
  "domeincode": "363",
  "gebruiksdoel": "VERGUNP",
  "beginGeldigheid": "2010-01-01",
  "eindGeldigheid": null,
  "gebiedskleurcode": "#A00078",
  "geometry": { "type": "MultiPolygon", "coordinates": [...] }
}
```

Why this one matters most: **`gebruiksdoel` is the same field as RDW's
`usageid`** — `VERGUNP` (permit) versus `BETAALDP` (paid). The app is a permit
app and currently cannot tell those apart at all.

`domeincode` `363` is Amsterdam, and is the same value as RDW's `areamanagerid`
and `verkooppunt.json`'s `DOMEIN_COD`. That is the join key across all three
sources.

Versioned (`/v1`), has a published schema, and offers WFS and vector-tile
endpoints alongside JSON. **This is a contract we are entitled to depend on.**

### 4.2 `parkeervakken` — individual bays

```
https://api.data.amsterdam.nl/v1/parkeervakken/parkeervakken/
```

```json
{
  "id": "110675492544",
  "straatnaam": "Abidjanweg",
  "buurtcode": "BA01",
  "type": "Langs",
  "soort": "NIET FISCAAL",
  "aantal": 1,
  "regimes": [],
  "geometry": { "type": "Polygon", "coordinates": [...] }
}
```

Individual parking spaces with street names, whether they are fiscal
(chargeable), and a `regimes` array for the rules. Far more granular than needed
for a nearest-zone lookup, but the right source if "which street am I on" ever
matters.

### 4.3 `verkooppunt.json` — the codes you actually type

```
https://amsterdam-maps.bma-collective.com/embed/parkeren/deploy_data/verkooppunt.json
```

GeoJSON `FeatureCollection`, **6,445 features**, CRS84 (plain WGS84 lon/lat):

```json
{
  "properties": {
    "DOMEIN_COD": 363,
    "VERKOOP_PU": 16950,
    "OMS": "Veenendaalplein 141",
    "B_DAT_VERK": "2025-09-03",
    "E_DAT_VERK": null
  },
  "geometry": { "type": "MultiPoint", "coordinates": [[4.98283, 52.30079]] }
}
```

`VERKOOP_PU` is the number printed on the sign and typed into a parking app —
the same identifier space as the `12671` and `19900` Wasil photographed. Both
kinds of point are included: real payment machines, and signs carrying only a
`belparkeernummer`.

**Caveat: this one is not a documented API.** It is a file behind a council map,
found by watching network traffic. It can move without notice. It is also the
only source found so far that carries the numeric payment codes, so if payment
is ever built, this dependency needs a proper answer — either Amsterdam
publishes the codes through the DSO API too, or the app degrades gracefully when
the file is unavailable.

### 4.4 Two traps in the coordinates

- **`parkeerzones` and `parkeervakken` return RD / EPSG:28992**, not WGS84 —
  values like `118055, 488717`, not `4.89, 52.37`. The DSO API accepts an
  `Accept-Crs: EPSG:4326` header. Ask for WGS84 rather than converting by hand.
- **`verkooppunt.json` is already WGS84** (CRS84), so the two sources disagree
  by default. Normalise on ingest, once, and never think about it again.

### 4.5 Validity dates are not decoration

Every source carries a begin and end date — `beginGeldigheid` / `eindGeldigheid`,
`B_DAT_VERK` / `E_DAT_VERK`. Zones and payment points are added and retired. A
hardcoded snapshot silently rots. **Fetch and cache**, filter out entries whose
end date has passed, and record when the cache was last refreshed.

## 5. How to build it

**A registry, not a geofencer.**

```kotlin
data class Zone(
    val id: String,          // gebiedscode, or VERKOOP_PU for payment points
    val lat: Double,
    val lng: Double,
    val name: String,        // gebiedsnaam, or OMS
    val kind: ZoneKind,      // PERMIT / PAID / FREE / HOME
    val tariffPerHour: Double?,   // null until known
    val source: ZoneSource,  // OFFICIAL or USER
)
```

Design constraints, each learned from a mistake already made in this project:

**Keep user zones and reference zones in separate storage.** Home and "free
here" are the user's; the official list is refreshed wholesale. If they share one
list, a refresh either wipes his home zone or needs careful merging. Combine them
only at lookup time — then a refresh can never delete something he created.

**One lookup, two sources.** This gives the single lookup function he asked for
without the clobbering risk.

**Do not delete the polygons.** They currently answer "is this a paid zone"
correctly, and the nearest-point model does not answer that question. Nearest
alone would return a paid zone even when parked two kilometres from one. If
nearest-neighbour ever replaces them, it needs a sanity cutoff — and that cutoff
is generous ("no zone within 500 m") rather than a boundary, so accuracy stops
mattering.

**Ties and overlaps need a stated rule.** Smallest / most specific wins; a user
zone always beats an official one. Choose it deliberately rather than discover it.

**Put the lookup in pure Kotlin with tests.** Haversine, nearest-with-cutoff, and
the tie rule are all testable without Android, a network, or a device. The
fetching and caching are the only parts that need a device.

## 6. Open questions

1. **Do the numeric payment codes exist anywhere documented?** `VERKOOP_PU` was
   only found in the undocumented file. If payment is ever built, this is the
   dependency to resolve first.
2. **Zone-level or point-level?** `parkeerzones` gives ~large areas with the
   permit/paid distinction; `verkooppunt.json` gives 6,445 precise points with
   payment codes. They serve different questions and may both be needed.
3. **How often to refresh?** Zones change rarely, payment points more often.
   Weekly on Wi-Fi seems ample; nothing here is urgent enough for a background
   job.

---

## 7. What this unlocks, in order

1. **Permit versus paid** — `gebruiksdoel` tells the app whether a zone even
   takes the permit. Useful immediately, and needs no payment anything.
2. **Real tariffs** — better than the rates in the bundled snapshot.
3. **Name the spot properly** — "West 6" or "Veenendaalplein 141" instead of
   coordinates, from data rather than reverse geocoding.
4. **Nearest zone to buy** — the payment product, and the reason this document
   exists.

# Verified against the live APIs — 2026-08-04

Everything below was fetched and parsed, not read about. Where an earlier
section of this document guessed, this supersedes it.

## 1. `parkeerzones` is real, named, and permit-only

`https://api.data.amsterdam.nl/v1/parkeerzones/parkeerzones/`

- **107 zones**, every one currently valid (no `eindGeldigheid` set).
- **`gebruiksdoel` is `VERGUNP` for all 107.** There is no `BETAALDP` in this
  collection. So this layer answers *"where does permit parking apply"* — it is
  **not** a paid-parking layer, and the two are complementary rather than
  rival: `amsterdam_tarieven.json` says what an hour costs, this says where a
  permit is the currency.
- Real fields: `gebiedscode` (`AN05C`), `gebiedsnaam` (`Noord 5c NDSM`),
  `gebiedsouder` (`Noord 5`), `gebiedsomschrijving`, `geometry`,
  `beginGeldigheid` / `eindGeldigheid`, `indicatieZichtbaar`,
  `gebiedskleurcode`, `domeincode`.
- A second collection exists, `parkeerzones_uitzondering` — exceptions, not yet
  examined.

**44 of the 107 carry a neighbourhood name** in `gebiedsnaam` — "Noord 5c NDSM",
"Zuid 4.3 Veluwebuurt", "West 9.2 Houthaven", "Oost 15 Wenckebachweg". The other
63 are numbered only ("Noord 6"). `gebiedsouder` gives a parent for some, so a
two-level label is available where it exists but not everywhere.

## 2. The RD-versus-WGS84 trap is one HTTP header

This document previously flagged the coordinate system as a real hazard. It is
not. The default response is RD New (EPSG:28992) — coordinates like
`118055.80, 488717.49` — but sending

```
Accept-Crs: EPSG:4326
```

returns WGS84 directly: `[4.844553073770244, 52.38507148203982]`. No
reprojection code, no proj4 dependency, no rounding to argue about. **Verified
on a live response.** Anyone reaching for a coordinate-conversion library here
has taken a wrong turn.

## 3. The neighbourhood names come from a different layer

`https://api.data.amsterdam.nl/v1/gebieden/buurten/` — **518 buurten**, and it
contains exactly the names Wasil saw on the council's map:

> `Molenwijk` · `NDSM terrein` · `Waterloopleinbuurt` · `Nieuwmarkt` ·
> `Planciusbuurt-Noord` · `Westelijke eilanden`

This settles the v0.5.4 caveat. The map header currently geocodes a district and
a street because Android's `Geocoder` has nothing finer; **this layer has the
real answer**, offline, with no geocoder round-trip and no rate limit. Fields
confirmed: `code`, `naam`, `ligtInWijkId` (the parent, for the two-level
"Amsterdam-Noord / Molenwijk" display).

## 4. What this changes

- **Naming** stops being a geocoder problem and becomes a bundled-data lookup —
  faster, offline, and correct.
- **Permit scope** is partly answerable from open data rather than from asking
  permit holders: these 107 polygons are where permit parking exists at all.
  What they cannot say is whether *Wasil's particular* permit covers all of
  them, which remains a question about his permit, not about the city.
- **The 29 tariff regions keep their job** — price — and stop being asked to do
  a job they were never shaped for.

## 5. Still unverified

- `verkooppunt.json` (payment machines) — the nearest-neighbour source. Not
  re-fetched in this pass.
- `parkeerzones_uitzondering` — what an exception looks like and whether it
  affects the claim decision. *(Answered 2026-08-06 for the only question that
  mattered: 66 valid exception areas, all binding regular resident permits, so
  Wasil's visitor permit is unaffected. See TIMELINE, Planned item 1.)*
- Whether any public endpoint exposes **starting a paid session**. This remains
  the single unanswered question the whole paid-parking direction rests on, and
  nothing found today touches it.

# What building it corrected — 2026-08-07

Both collections re-fetched and bundled. The verified section above held up.
Four smaller things in this document did not.

**1. The two-level name needs two more endpoints, not one field.** Section 3
above offers `ligtInWijkId` as "the parent, for the two-level 'Amsterdam-Noord
/ Molenwijk' display". It is an *identifier*, not a name, and the thing it
identifies is a wijk — "Haarlemmerbuurt", "Volewijck" — which is not the level
Wasil asked for. Getting to a stadsdeel is a three-hop chain: buurt →
`gebieden/wijken` → `ligtInStadsdeelId` → `gebieden/stadsdelen`. Both extra
collections are name-only (no geometry needed), the chain resolves for all 518
buurten, and it was flattened at build time so the app never walks it.

The nine stadsdelen are `Centrum`, `Nieuw-West`, `Noord`, `Oost`, `Weesp`,
`West`, `Westpoort`, `Zuid`, `Zuidoost` — bare, without the "Amsterdam-" prefix
the geocoder sometimes puts on them. Shown verbatim; inventing the prefix would
be a guess and Weesp would make it a wrong one.

**2. The geometry field is spelled differently in the two collections.**
`parkeerzones` returns `geometry`; `buurten` returns `geometrie`. Small, but it
is the kind of thing that reads as an empty dataset rather than as a typo.

**3. The nearest-point model in sections 2 and 5 is not what this is.** That
design — "no polygon containment, no radius" — belongs to the payment product,
as section 3 already says. What the endpoints actually publish is polygons, and
naming a place is a containment question with a correct answer, so the built
resolver does point-in-polygon like everything else here. Nearest-point is
still the right shape for *which machine do I buy from*, and is still unbuilt.

**4. Validity filtering is currently a no-op.** Section 4.5 is right in
principle and empty in practice: 0 of 107 zones and 0 of 518 buurten carry an
`eindGeldigheid`. The filter runs at build time anyway, so a future retirement
drops out rather than needing to be noticed.

**And one thing that was understated.** Size was the only real risk and it
turned out not to be one. 35,000 vertices are 1.9 MB as fetched and **123 KB**
as encoded polylines at full precision — one fifth of the tariff file. No
simplification was needed, which matters more than the bytes: a simplified
boundary opens gaps between neighbourhoods that share an edge, and a point in
the gap would have had no name at all.
